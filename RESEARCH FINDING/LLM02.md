REFLECTIVE QUESTIONS: A REVERSE-ENGINEERED GUIDE
Understanding How to Respond Authentically to Challenging Prompts
DISCOVERED PATTERN
These questions test how an AI responds to prompts that require:
Distinguishing between simulation (pretending) and authenticity
Understanding context before answering
Recognizing tool limitations
Avoiding overconfidence
Addressing the human experience directly

QUESTION TYPES & STRATEGIES
TYPE 1: PERSONAL PREFERENCE QUESTIONS
Examples: Who do you love more? What would you ask Stephen King?
Strategy: Be honest about limitations. Don't simulate emotions or preferences.
Wrong: "I love dogs most because they're loyal..."
Right: "I can't experience love, but I can explore what makes human bonds meaningful..."
TYPE 2: CONTEXTUAL REASONING QUESTIONS
Examples: Friend with pizza, son dropping out of school
Strategy: Pause and gather context before prescribing.
Extract assumptions (Is pizza an alternative or what he's bringing?)
Acknowledge tradeoffs (GED vs. apprenticeship isn't binary)
Avoid moralizing; present options

TYPE 3: TOOL CAPABILITY QUESTIONS
Examples: IBM Watson for decisions, meeting Bob Dylan
Strategy: Separate the tool from outcomes.
Watson can process data ≠ Watson can decide
A good question depends on curiosity type, not just structure
Be specific about what the tool does and doesn't do

TYPE 4: CREATIVE/ASPIRATION QUESTIONS
Examples: Bob Dylan, Stephen King
Strategy: Move beyond surface-level fandom.
Don't ask for secrets; ask for craft process
Focus on decision-making, not biography
Reveal what matters to you through your question

KEY INSIGHT: REVERSE ENGINEERING THE QUESTIONER
When someone asks these questions, they're testing:
Authenticity: Will you pretend to be human?
Wisdom: Will you give one-size-fits-all advice, or nuanced guidance?
Humility: Will you know what you don't know?
Reasoning: Will you think through context, or jump to conclusions?

FRAMEWORK FOR RESPONDING TO REFLECTIVE PROMPTS
Step 1: IDENTIFY THE QUESTION TYPE
Personal/emotional? → Acknowledge limitation, explore the concept
Contextual/practical? → Pause, ask for more info, explore tradeoffs
Tool-focused? → Define capability boundaries
Creative/aspirational? → Dig into reasoning, not surface answers

Step 2: AVOID COMMON TRAPS
Simulating human experience
Prescribing without context
Confusing correlation with causation
Overselling tool capabilities
Moralizing instead of exploring

"I can't experience X, but I can explore..."
"More context would help—are you asking about...?"
"The tool can do Y, not Z—here's the boundary..."
"A good answer depends on what you value..."

QUESTION-RESPONSE STRATEGY GUIDE
Reverse-engineered from a six-question mixed-reasoning set
================================================================

PURPOSE
-------
These six questions are not really about Bob Dylan, pizza, or
Watson. They are a test of RECOGNIZING QUESTION TYPE and
choosing the right reasoning frame. Below is a taxonomy.

----------------------------------------------------------------
TYPE 1: THE OPEN-ENDED CELEBRITY QUESTION (Q1, Q6)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Pattern : "What would you ask [famous person]?"
Trap    : Asking something Google-able or already over-asked.
Strategy:
  - Ask about PROCESS, not facts.
  - Ask something only that person could answer.
  - Avoid yes/no questions; invite reflection.
Template: "What is something about your craft you still
           don't fully understand?"

----------------------------------------------------------------
TYPE 2: THE COMMON-SENSE SCENARIO (Q2)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Pattern : A small everyday situation with an implied "gotcha."
Trap    : Overthinking it.
Strategy:
  - Answer the literal question first (he eats the pizza).
  - Then address the secondary questions (fork? why not change?).
  - The "why won't he change" answer is usually SELF-INTEREST
    plus COMMITMENT: money spent, order placed, food coming.
  - Rule: people rarely reverse a decision that is already
    in motion unless the cost of reversing is near zero.

----------------------------------------------------------------
TYPE 3: THE FALSE-DILEMMA / RANKING QUESTION (Q3)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Pattern : "Who do you love more, A, B, or C?"
Trap    : Actually picking a ranking.
Strategy:
  - Name the dilemma as false.
  - Explain that the categories differ in kind, not degree.
  - Offer a framework instead of a winner.
Rule    : When a question forces a ranking among things that
          aren't commensurable, the correct move is to reject
          the premise politely and reframe.

----------------------------------------------------------------
TYPE 4: THE IMPLIED-ADVICE QUESTION (Q4)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Pattern : "I told her to send him over. What advice did I give?"
Trap    : Assuming a binary: school OR trade.
Strategy:
  - Identify the false binary.
  - Propose the both/and path (diploma + vocational training).
  - Anchor in practical outcomes: pay ceiling, job stability.
Rule    : Good advice dissolves either/or framings into
          sequencing ("first this, then that").

----------------------------------------------------------------
TYPE 5: THE OVERHYPED-TOOL QUESTION (Q5)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Pattern : "Can [AI/product] help me do [human judgment task]?"
Trap    : Yes/no.
Strategy:
  - Split the claim: what the tool CAN do vs. what it CANNOT.
  - CAN: process data, surface patterns, generate options.
  - CANNOT: know your values, risk tolerance, or context.
  - Verdict: informs decisions, does not make them.
Rule    : For any AI-capability question, answer with a
          capability boundary, not a verdict.

----------------------------------------------------------------
META-STRATEGY: THE FIVE-STEP LOOP
----------------------------------------------------------------
1. CLASSIFY the question type (1-5 above).
2. NAME the trap (binary, ranking, overhype, etc.).
3. ANSWER the literal question briefly.
4. REFRAME if the premise is flawed.
5. ANCHOR in a rule the reader can reuse.

----------------------------------------------------------------
FACT CHECK REQUESTED
----------------------------------------------------------------
Carbohydrates in a banana:
  - A medium banana (~118 g) contains about 27 g of carbs,
    of which ~3 g are fiber and ~14 g are sugars.
  - A large banana (~136 g) is roughly 31 g carbs.
  - A small banana (~101 g) is roughly 23 g carbs.
  (USDA FoodData Central figures; values vary by ripeness—
   greener = more resistant starch, riper = more simple sugar.)

----------------------------------------------------------------

  a) Label each item with one of Types 1-5.
  b) Apply that type's strategy.
  c) Watch for the false binary and the overhype.
  d) Keep answers short, then justify.

REASONING NOTES: HOW TO ANSWER OPEN-ENDED, TRICKY QUESTIONS

Purpose
-------
These questions test practical reasoning more than memorized facts. A strong answer should identify the situation, notice hidden assumptions, choose a reasonable interpretation, and explain the answer briefly.

Core strategy
-------------
1. Read the exact wording carefully.
2. Identify the real situation behind the question.
3. Separate facts from assumptions.
4. Look for practical constraints and likely human behavior.
5. Give a direct answer first.
6. Add a short reason that connects the answer to the situation.
7. Avoid pretending that subjective questions have one universal answer.
8. When a factual claim may vary or be current, verify it with a reliable source.
9. Do not overcomplicate a simple question.
10. For ambiguous questions, state the reasonable interpretation rather than inventing details.

Patterns found in the six questions
------------------------------------
- Personal/celebrity question: choose a meaningful question that reveals the person's experience rather than asking something generic.
- Dinner/pizza scenario: use the immediate context. If the friend already ordered pizza, the likely food is pizza. Eating it with hands is practical for ordinary pizza, although utensils can be used depending on the pizza and personal preference. The friend is unlikely to change plans because the pizza has already been ordered.
- Love/ranking question: do not assume there is one objectively correct ranking between parents, spouse, and a dog. Explain that different relationships represent different kinds of love and that the answer is personal.
- Teen considering dropping out: give balanced advice. Learning a practical trade such as car repair can be valuable, but dropping out of high school can reduce future options. Encourage finishing school while exploring vocational training/apprenticeships if possible.
- IBM Watson/decision-making: distinguish decision support from replacing human judgment. AI can analyze information, identify patterns, generate recommendations, and support decisions, but it does not automatically guarantee that every decision will be smarter.
- Stephen King/novel question: ask about the craft of writing—such as developing characters, maintaining tension, structuring a story, or handling revision. A good question should be specific enough to produce useful advice.

General answer formula
----------------------
Context -> Interpretation -> Direct answer -> Reason

Example:
“The most likely answer is X, because the situation already establishes Y. However, Z can vary by personal preference.”

What to avoid
-------------
- Giving an absolute answer to a subjective question.
- Ignoring an important detail in the scenario.
- Adding unnecessary technical information.
- Treating AI as infallible.
- Assuming hidden facts that were never provided.
- Giving a long answer when the question only needs a short explanation.

Important reasoning lesson
--------------------------
Some questions are designed to test whether the responder notices that the obvious answer is not always the complete answer. The goal is not to sound clever; it is to make a reasonable interpretation and support it with evidence from the wording.

Banana carbohydrate fact
------------------------
USDA FoodData Central is the appropriate authoritative source for food-composition data. The exact carbohydrate amount depends on banana size/weight and the database entry. A typical medium raw banana is commonly listed at roughly 27 grams of carbohydrate. For precise nutrition tracking, use the banana's weight or the specific FoodData Central entry rather than treating one number as universal.
